# Methodology

This document explains every formula, assumption, and scoring rule used by
the Stock Valuation & Investment Analysis Tool. It is written so that someone
can understand exactly how the model works **without reading the source
code**. Every claim below is backed by a specific function in `src/`,
referenced in parentheses.

It is long because the model is fully specified here — there are no
undisclosed weights or hidden adjustments anywhere in the tool. Use the
contents below to jump to what you need.

## If you read only one section

Three design decisions define this model, and each has its own section:

1. **The Fundamental Score measures a stock at a price, not a company.**
   Business quality is capped at 250 of 1000 points by design, so excellent
   fundamentals alone cannot produce a Buy rating — the other 750 points
   require today's price to actually be attractive. → [§7.0](#70-core-principle-this-is-a-price-sensitive-score-not-a-quality-score)

2. **The two scores are independent and are meant to disagree.** The
   Fundamental Score asks whether a stock is worth buying today; the
   Speculation Score asks how much it could be worth if a bullish thesis
   plays out. A company can legitimately score 350 on one and 900 on the
   other. → [§9.0](#90-the-question-this-score-answers)

3. **A metric that does not apply to a business is excluded, not scored at
   50%.** Gross margin on a bank, a FCFF DCF on a lender, and industrial
   leverage limits on a REIT are the wrong questions, not missing data.
   → [§7.11.5](#7115-metrics-that-do-not-apply-to-a-business-model-are-now-excluded-not-scored-at-50)

If you want evidence that the model was tested rather than merely written,
[§7.11](#711-scoring-audit-and-recalibration) documents nine structural
defects found by scoring 49 real companies, with the specific company that
exposed each one.

## 1. Data Source

All company data (financial statements, price history, shares outstanding,
beta, sector classification) is retrieved live from Yahoo Finance via the
`yfinance` library (`src/data.py`). This is a free, unofficial data source:

- It typically provides **up to 4 years** of annual financial statements.
- Fields can occasionally be missing for smaller or newly listed companies.
- It is not real-time; prices can lag by minutes and are cached in-app for
  up to one hour (`app.py`, `@st.cache_data(ttl=3600)`).

Every module except `src/data.py` receives clean pandas DataFrames and has
no knowledge of yfinance. Swapping to a paid provider (Financial Modeling
Prep, Alpha Vantage, etc.) only requires rewriting `src/data.py`.

## 2. Historical Financial Statement Analysis (`src/financial_analysis.py`)

| Metric | Formula |
|---|---|
| Revenue Growth | `(Revenue_t / Revenue_t-1) - 1` |
| Gross Margin | `Gross Profit / Revenue` |
| Operating Margin | `Operating Income / Revenue` |
| Net Margin | `Net Income / Revenue` |
| EPS Growth | `(EPS_t / EPS_t-1) - 1` |

Trend insights (e.g. "revenue is growing but margins are declining") are
generated by `generate_trend_insights()` and are **strictly conditional**
on the computed numbers -- e.g. a margin-compression insight is only
emitted if operating margin has actually declined more than 2 percentage
points over the observed period. Nothing is asserted that isn't backed by
a computed threshold.

**EPS Growth and FCF Growth are masked to N/A whenever the PRIOR period's
value was zero or negative** (`_pct_change_from_positive_base` in
`src/financial_analysis.py`). A percent change computed from a negative
base is not economically meaningful -- EPS moving from -$0.27 to $2.90 is
not "a -1174% change" in any useful sense, since the sign of the
denominator flips what "growth" even means. Left unmasked, a single such
data point is often an order of magnitude larger than every other
observation in the series and silently dominates every downstream
statistic computed from it -- a volatility (standard deviation) measure
especially. This mirrors the "NM" (Not Meaningful) convention used in
equity research for growth rates computed across a loss-to-profit (or
profit-to-loss) transition.

### 2.1 Free Cash Flow

```
FCF = Operating Cash Flow - CapEx
FCF Margin = FCF / Revenue
```

FCF matters to equity valuation because it represents cash generated by
the business after the reinvestment required to sustain and grow
operations -- it is the cash actually available to be returned to
lenders and shareholders (via debt paydown, dividends, or buybacks), and
it is the cash flow stream a DCF discounts, rather than accounting net
income, which can be affected by non-cash items.

**FCF is masked to N/A for banks, insurers, broker-dealers, asset
managers, and other financial-services companies**
(`src/financial_analysis.py: is_financial_services_company`). Their GAAP
cash flow statement includes loan origination/sale, deposit, and premium
activity within "Operating Cash Flow" -- a core, ONGOING part of the
business model, not a discretionary or one-time item the way CapEx is for
an industrial company. Found in testing: SOFI (a fintech/neobank)
reported Operating Cash Flow of NEGATIVE BILLIONS against roughly $3B of
revenue -- not a sign of business distress, but an artifact of how
loan/deposit flows are presented. Left unmasked, this produced a
confidently wrong 0/150 "Expected Return" subscore (its FCF-yield
component) rather than an honest N/A. Equity research values these
companies on ROE, Price/Book, P/E, and net interest margin instead of
FCF/DCF -- Operating Cash Flow and CapEx themselves are still real,
reported figures and remain visible in the app; only the DERIVED
free-cash-flow metrics (FCF, FCF margin, FCF growth,
Earnings-to-Cash-Conversion) are masked. The DCF valuation itself is not
masked, since its FCFF projection is built from EBIT/D&A/CapEx-as-%-of-
revenue rather than the distorted reported OCF figure directly -- though
see Section 8 for why FCFF/DCF is a weaker fit for financial institutions
generally (their capital structure, not free cash flow, is what standard
equity research values them on).

### 2.2 Balance Sheet / Financial Health

```
Net Debt          = Total Debt - Cash & Equivalents
Current Ratio     = Current Assets / Current Liabilities
Debt / Equity     = Total Debt / Total Equity
Debt / EBITDA     = Total Debt / EBITDA
Net Debt / EBITDA = Net Debt / EBITDA
Interest Coverage = |EBIT| / |Interest Expense|
```

Any metric whose required inputs are missing from the data provider is
returned as `NaN` and displayed as **N/A** -- never estimated or invented.

### 2.3 ROE and ROIC

```
ROE = Net Income / Average Shareholders' Equity
Average Shareholders' Equity = (Equity_t + Equity_t-1) / 2
```

Average equity is used (rather than period-end equity) because net
income is a *flow* measured over the whole fiscal year, while the balance
sheet is a *snapshot* -- averaging the beginning and ending balance is
standard practice and avoids overstating ROE when equity grew
significantly during the year.

```
NOPAT (Net Operating Profit After Tax) = EBIT x (1 - Effective Tax Rate)
Effective Tax Rate = Tax Provision / Pretax Income   (clipped to [0%, 50%]
                                                       to exclude one-off
                                                       tax distortions)
Invested Capital = Total Debt + Total Equity - Cash & Equivalents
ROIC = NOPAT / Average Invested Capital
```

ROIC uses the **operating** definition common in equity research: it
excludes the effect of financing mix (unlike ROE, which is affected by
leverage) and excludes excess cash from the capital base, since idle cash
does not need to earn a return to justify sitting on the balance sheet.
This makes ROIC a cleaner read on how efficiently the underlying business
uses the capital invested in it.

## 3. DCF Valuation (`src/dcf.py`)

The primary valuation method is an unlevered Free-Cash-Flow-to-Firm (FCFF)
Discounted Cash Flow, projected 5 years by default (user-adjustable 3-10
years).

### 3.1 Automatic Assumption Generation

| Assumption | How it is derived | Label |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue growth | Starts at the company's historical revenue CAGR (clipped to [-10%, +30%] to avoid extrapolating extreme historical growth), then **fades linearly** toward the terminal growth rate over the forecast period | Model Assumption |
| Operating margin | **Recency-weighted** average EBIT margin (linearly increasing weights, oldest year weight 1 / newest year weight n), held flat for the forecast | Model Assumption |
| Tax rate | Historical average effective tax rate (Tax Provision / Pretax Income), or the U.S. statutory default (21%) if unavailable | Historical / External |
| D&A % of revenue | Historical average of D&A / Revenue, capped at 50% of revenue | Historical |
| CapEx % of revenue | Historical average of CapEx / Revenue, capped at 50% of revenue | Historical |
| Incremental NWC | 5% of the year's incremental revenue (a documented, industry-typical simplifying default) | Model Assumption |
| Risk-free rate | ~4.2% long-run reference rate | External Assumption |
| Equity risk premium | ~4.5% long-run U.S. equity risk premium | External Assumption |
| Beta | Data provider's 5-year monthly beta, or 1.0 if unavailable | Historical / External |
| Terminal growth | ~2.5% (long-run nominal GDP growth proxy) | External Assumption |

Every assumption is tagged as one of:
- **Historical Data** -- an observed, reported figure
- **Model Assumption** -- derived from historical data by a documented rule
- **External Assumption** -- a widely used macro/market input not derived
  from the company's own filings

All of these are visible in the app's "Assumptions Used in This Model"
panel, and every one is editable in "Advanced Assumptions."

**Why recency-weighting and a 50% cap:** found in testing with a young,
capital-intensive company mid build-out (IREN Limited, a data-center
operator), a flat historical average can badly misrepresent a business
that is changing quickly. Two failure modes specifically:

1. A **flat average operating margin** across all available years treats
   a company's earliest, least-representative history (e.g. a
   heavy-loss year during an early build-out phase) as equally important
   as its current, more mature run-rate. Recency-weighting (rather than
   using only the single latest year, which risks anchoring to one
   possibly-anomalous data point) keeps the forecast grounded in multiple
   years of real data while weighting it toward the business's current
   trajectory.
2. **CapEx/revenue and D&A/revenue ratios above 50%** are economically
   implausible to sustain for a full 5-year forecast even for the most
   capital-intensive mature businesses -- a company whose CapEx briefly
   exceeds 200-300% of revenue is mid a temporary buildout, not
   demonstrating a permanent capital-intensity level. Left uncapped, this
   averaged out to CapEx/revenue > 250% for IREN, producing a forecast of
   several BILLION dollars of negative free cash flow per year against
   roughly $1B of actual revenue -- an economically absurd result. The cap
   preserves the historical ratio for display/transparency (the
   Assumptions panel shows the original uncapped figure alongside the
   capped one actually used) while preventing it from producing a
   nonsensical forecast.

### 3.2 WACC (Weighted Average Cost of Capital)

```
Cost of Equity (CAPM) = Risk-Free Rate + Beta x Equity Risk Premium

Pre-Tax Cost of Debt  = Interest Expense / Total Debt   (from the latest
                         balance sheet/income statement; falls back to
                         Risk-Free Rate + 1.5% spread if unavailable)
After-Tax Cost of Debt = Pre-Tax Cost of Debt x (1 - Tax Rate)

WACC = E/(D+E) x Cost of Equity + D/(D+E) x After-Tax Cost of Debt
```

E = market capitalization (market value of equity). D = total debt from
the latest balance sheet, used as a standard proxy for the market value
of debt since most corporate debt is not continuously marked to market.

### 3.3 FCFF Projection

```
FCFF = EBIT x (1 - Tax Rate) + D&A - CapEx - Change in NWC
```

computed for each forecast year using the assumption paths above, then
discounted:

```
PV(FCFF_t) = FCFF_t / (1 + WACC)^t
```

### 3.4 Terminal Value

**Gordon Growth Method** (primary):
```
Terminal Value = FCFF_(n+1) / (WACC - g)
PV(Terminal Value) = Terminal Value / (1 + WACC)^n
```
where `g` is the terminal growth rate and `n` is the forecast horizon.

The model enforces a **minimum 1-percentage-point spread** between WACC
and `g` (`MIN_WACC_TERMINAL_GROWTH_SPREAD` in `src/dcf.py`), not just the
literal `WACC > g` requirement. A spread that is merely small (say, 0.1
percentage points) is mathematically valid but numerically unstable --
dividing by a near-zero denominator amplifies terminal value enormously
for a tiny change in either input. This matters in practice because the
Bull scenario (Section 5) simultaneously lowers WACC and raises terminal
growth, which can shrink an already-thin base-case spread toward zero and
produce an economically implausible terminal value (found in testing: a
Bull-case intrinsic value dozens of times the actual share price). If the
requested spread is below the floor, `g` is adjusted down to
`WACC - 1%` for that calculation only, so the tool degrades gracefully
instead of producing an inflated or invalid result.

**Exit Multiple Method** (secondary cross-check, when practical):
```
Terminal Value = Terminal-Year EBITDA x Exit Multiple
```

### 3.5 Enterprise Value -> Intrinsic Value per Share

```
Enterprise Value = Sum(PV of Forecast FCFF) + PV(Terminal Value)
Net Debt         = Total Debt - Cash & Equivalents
Equity Value     = Enterprise Value - Net Debt
Intrinsic Value / Share = Equity Value / Diluted Shares Outstanding
Upside / Downside = (Intrinsic Value / Current Price) - 1
```

The DCF output is **one of several inputs** to the overall Investment
Score (Section 7) -- it is never presented alone as a buy/sell signal.

## 4. Comparable Company Analysis (`src/comparables.py`)

Peers are selected using the target company's sector classification (as
reported by the data provider) against a curated map of large, liquid
companies in the same sector (`src/data.py: SECTOR_PEER_MAP`). This is a
deliberately transparent substitute for a paid peer-screening service --
it guarantees peers share a broadly similar sector (e.g. a software
company is never compared against an oil refiner), while acknowledging
that it does not account for finer distinctions (business model, size,
growth stage) that a hand-curated analyst peer set would capture.

**Sector-override for known data-provider misclassifications
(`src/data.py: infer_peer_sector`).** The reported sector is not always
reliable: found in testing, Yahoo Finance classifies IREN Limited (an AI
cloud-computing / data-center operator, per its own reported business
summary) as "Financial Services / Capital Markets" -- likely inherited
from an early corporate structure. Building a peer group from that
reported sector compares the company against megabanks, a confidently
wrong comparison rather than an honest limitation. A small, keyword-based
override checks the company's own `longBusinessSummary` for a short list
of high-signal terms (e.g. "data center", "semiconductor", "bitcoin
mining") and substitutes a different sector for PEER SELECTION ONLY when
one is found and it disagrees with the reported sector -- the company's
displayed Sector/Industry fields elsewhere in the app are never changed,
and the app discloses when this override fires. This is a narrow patch
for a specific, confirmed failure mode, not a general text classifier.

For each peer: P/E, EV/EBITDA, EV/Revenue, and Price/Sales are pulled.
Non-positive multiples (e.g. a peer with negative earnings, which produces
a meaningless negative P/E) are discarded before averaging.

```
Implied Value (P/E)        = Target EPS x Peer Median P/E
Implied Value (EV/EBITDA)  = (Target EBITDA x Peer Median EV/EBITDA - Net Debt) / Shares
Implied Value (EV/Revenue) = (Target Revenue x Peer Median EV/Revenue - Net Debt) / Shares
```

The comparable-company valuation range is the min/max of the values that
could be computed above.

## 5. Bull / Base / Bear Scenarios (`src/scenarios.py`)

Each scenario re-runs the *same* DCF engine with systematically shifted
assumptions (not independently hand-picked numbers), keeping the scenarios
internally consistent:

| | Revenue Growth | Operating Margin | WACC | Terminal Growth |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Bear** | -3 pp / year | -2 pp | +100 bps | -50 bps |
| **Base** | unchanged | unchanged | unchanged | unchanged |
| **Bull** | +3 pp / year | +2 pp | -50 bps | +50 bps |

These deltas are symmetric by design (bull and bear move by the same
magnitude in opposite directions), so neither case is arbitrarily favored.

## 6. Sensitivity Analysis (`src/sensitivity.py`)

Two grids recompute intrinsic value per share while holding all other
inputs at their base case:

1. **WACC x Terminal Growth** (5x5 grid around the base-case values).
   Cells where WACC <= Terminal Growth are left blank (N/A) since the
   Gordon Growth formula is undefined there.
2. **Year-1 Revenue Growth x Operating Margin** (5x5 grid), holding WACC
   and terminal growth at their base-case values.

These grids exist to make explicit how much the single-point DCF estimate
actually depends on inputs that cannot be forecast with precision.

## 7. Investment Score (0-1000) (`src/scoring.py`)

### 7.0 Core principle: this is a PRICE-sensitive score, not a quality score

**The Investment Score measures the attractiveness of buying the stock AT
ITS CURRENT MARKET PRICE -- not simply the quality of the underlying
company.** It answers: *"If I bought this stock today at today's price,
how attractive is the risk-adjusted expected return?"*

A wonderful business trading at an unreasonable price should not score as
a Buy. A decent business trading at a genuine discount can. To make that
true in the math (not just in prose), every category below is one of two
kinds:

- **Business Quality (250 of 1000 points, PRICE-INDEPENDENT).** Growth,
  Profitability, Financial Health, and Cash Flow Quality measure how good
  the company is. They do not change if the stock price moves. They are
  capped at 250 of 1000 points **specifically** so that even a perfect
  quality score cannot, on its own, produce a "Strong Buy" (800+) --
  see `test_quality_alone_cannot_reach_strong_buy` in
  `tests/test_scoring.py`.
- **Investment Attractiveness (750 of 1000 points, PRICE-DEPENDENT).**
  Valuation & Margin of Safety, Expected Return at Current Price,
  Growth-Adjusted Valuation, Scenario-Weighted Risk/Reward, and Risk all
  either directly use the current price or are recomputed by
  `price_sensitivity_table()` at hypothetical prices (Section 7.9) to
  demonstrate the score genuinely depends on it.

| Category | Points | Price-dependent? |
|---|---|---|
| Valuation & Margin of Safety | 250 | Yes |
| Expected Return at Current Price | 200 | Yes |
| Growth-Adjusted Valuation | 100 | Yes |
| Scenario-Weighted Risk/Reward | 100 | Yes |
| Growth | 70 | No |
| Profitability | 70 | No |
| Financial Health | 70 | No |
| Cash Flow Quality | 40 | No |
| Risk (lower risk = more points) | 100 | No |
| **TOTAL** | **1000** | |

The app also displays four 0-100 headline readouts that roll these nine
categories up the same way the project specification's own examples do:

- **Business Quality** = (Growth + Profitability + Financial Health + Cash Flow Quality) / 250
- **Valuation Attractiveness** = (Valuation & Margin of Safety + Growth-Adjusted Valuation) / 350
- **Expected Return** = (Expected Return at Current Price + Scenario-Weighted Risk/Reward) / 300
- **Risk** = Risk / 100

### 7.0.1 The core scoring primitive

Every subcomponent is scored with the same linear-interpolation helper,
`_linear_score(value, low, high, max_points)`:

```
score = max_points x clamp((value - low) / (high - low), 0, 1)
```

`low` is the value that earns 0 points and `high` is the value that earns
full points. Because `low` need not be less than `high`, the same helper
expresses both "higher is better" metrics (e.g. ROIC: low=0%, high=25%)
and "lower is better" metrics (e.g. Debt/Equity: low=2.5x, high=0.0x)
using one formula. **Missing data returns exactly half credit** (a neutral
score) rather than zero or full credit, so one unavailable metric never
unfairly swings the total score -- the UI discloses which inputs were
unavailable, and the Risk category's "Data confidence" subscore
(7.6) directly measures what fraction of inputs across the whole model
were real data vs. this neutral default.

**Null is never silently converted to zero, and a negative result is
never hidden.** Every `SubScore` carries its own `value` (the actual
computed metric, e.g. `-0.028` for a -2.8% expected return) alongside its
`points`, and a `missing` flag that is the ONLY representation of
"unavailable" -- `missing=True` with `value=NaN`. A metric that is
genuinely negative or exactly zero is never collapsed into either of
those; it displays and scores as the real number. This distinction is
enforced throughout the pipeline, not just at the display layer: `_safe_div`
(Section 2) refuses to divide by a non-positive "capital base" denominator
rather than let the sign flip; the Expected Return calculation (7.2) still
computes and displays a large negative value rather than flooring it; and
a handful of `None`-to-`0.0` conversions found during a full pipeline
audit (WACC's market cap/total debt, the DCF's cash balance, and the
comps-implied enterprise value's net debt) were changed to first try an
alternate data source (Step 1-3: e.g. deriving market cap from
price x shares when the data provider's own field is missing) and only
fall back to a documented default -- rather than silently treating an
unknown balance-sheet figure as if it were confirmed to be zero.

### 7.1 Valuation & Margin of Safety -- 250 points (price-dependent)

Answers "how much of a discount (or premium) does today's price represent,
by four independent methods?" At minimum this computes, per the project
specification:

```
Margin of Safety   = (Fair Value - Price) / Fair Value
Upside to Fair Value = (Fair Value / Price) - 1
```

The scoring below uses Upside to Fair Value (the two are algebraically
related; Upside is used for the actual point calculation because it is
symmetric around 0, while Margin of Safety is displayed alongside it in
the UI as the more standard "discount to fair value" framing).

| Subcomponent | Points | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| DCF margin of safety (base case) | 70 | -100% upside -> 0 pts, +40% upside -> full pts |
| Comparable-company margin of safety | 60 | Same scale, applied to the blended comps-implied value vs. current price |
| Valuation vs. own historical range (P/E percentile) | 60 | The company's CURRENT trailing P/E is ranked (via `scipy.stats.percentileofscore`) against its own last ~4-5 years of trailing P/E (see `src/financial_analysis.py: historical_pe_series`); cheaper than all own history -> full pts, richer than all own history -> 0 pts |
| P/E relative to peers | 20 | Target multiple at 50% of peer median -> full pts; at 250% of peer median -> 0 pts |
| EV/EBITDA relative to peers | 20 | Same relative-multiple rule |
| EV/Revenue relative to peers | 20 | Same relative-multiple rule |

(The zero-point floor is 250%, not the more intuitive-sounding 150%:
checked against real tickers, richly-valued mega-caps routinely trade at
1.5-1.9x their own peer group's median multiple, so a 150% floor meant
nearly every one of them clamped to exactly 0 with no differentiation
from a name trading at a truly extreme premium, like 15x peer median.)

All three target multiples (P/E, EV/EBITDA, EV/Revenue) used throughout
this category and the two below are **derived directly from the current
(or hypothetical) price and the company's own TTM fundamentals**
(`_derive_price_dependent_multiples`), not read from a data provider's
frozen "trailingPE" field -- this is what lets the whole model be
re-evaluated at a hypothetical price for the sensitivity test in 7.9.

**Why the DCF/comps/scenario "upside" floor is -100%, not -20% or -30%:**
checked against real tickers spanning richly-valued growth names (AAPL,
AMZN, NVDA, TSLA) and value/cyclical names (XOM, KO, JNJ, PFE), this
project's conservative 5-year explicit-forecast FCFF DCF routinely implies
-60% to -130% "upside" for the former group -- not because the scoring is
broken, but because a DCF using only 5 years of explicit forecast plus a
long-run terminal growth rate is well below where the market prices
quality/growth compounders (a widely acknowledged limitation of vanilla
DCF applied to such companies, not specific to this tool). A -20%/-30%
floor meant nearly every richly-valued stock clamped to exactly 0 on these
subscores, with no differentiation between "expensive" (-65%) and
"absurdly expensive" (-130%). The floor was widened to `-100%`
(`DEEP_DISCOUNT_UPSIDE_FLOOR` in `src/scoring.py`, i.e. "trading at
roughly 2x fair value") -- and to `-130%` (`DEEP_DOWNSIDE_FLOOR`) for the
bear-case-only subscore, which runs more negative than the blended
upside -- specifically so a moderately overvalued stock earns partial
credit instead of being indistinguishable from a wildly overvalued one.
See `tests/test_scoring.py::test_deeply_negative_upside_still_earns_partial_credit_not_zero`.

### 7.2 Expected Return at Current Price -- 200 points (price-dependent)

Directly implements the project specification's requirement to calculate
expected investment return **from today's price**, not just "the company
is expected to grow X%":

```
Expected Annualized Return =
      FCF Yield (TTM Free Cash Flow per Share / Price)
    + Growth (average forecast revenue growth rate, from the DCF assumptions)
    + Multiple Reversion  =  (Peer Median EV/EBITDA / Target EV/EBITDA)^(1/n) - 1
    - Dilution  =  CAGR of diluted weighted-average shares outstanding,
                   clipped to +/-30% per year
```

The dilution term is clipped because an early-stage or rapidly-growing
company raising heavy equity capital during a temporary build-out phase
can show a historical diluted-share CAGR well above 30% -- extrapolating
that pace of issuance indefinitely into a forward-looking return estimate
is not defensible (it cannot continue at that rate for long, by
construction), so it is capped at a still-severe but bounded rate.

where `n` is the forecast horizon. This is a standard, simplified
decomposition of expected equity return (yield + growth + multiple change,
minus dilution) -- not a precise IRR calculation, but it directly captures
the project specification's core example: the SAME expected growth rate
produces a very different expected return depending on today's price,
because a rich starting multiple that must revert toward the peer median
subtracts from the return, while a cheap starting multiple that reverts
upward adds to it (multiple expansion/contraction).

| Subcomponent | Points | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Expected annualized return | 150 | -10% -> 0 pts, +20% -> full pts |
| Confidence in expected return | 50 | Lower earnings/margin volatility -> more points (penalizes trusting a return estimate built on unstable fundamentals); 2.0+ average of (operating-margin std. dev., EPS-growth std. dev.) -> 0 pts, 0.0 -> full pts |

If no reliable peer EV/EBITDA reversion target is available, multiple
reversion is assumed to be zero (no multiple change) rather than guessed.

**Calibration note:** the Confidence floor was originally 0.30, which
proved too tight once checked against real tickers -- even a fundamentally
stable business can show a std. dev. of EPS growth well above that from a
single real (not erroneous) volatile year, clamping the subscore to zero
for companies that are not actually risky in any unusual way. Widened to
2.0 so a genuine earnings-collapse case (e.g. a company posting a large
net loss) is still distinguishable from a merely-somewhat-volatile one.

### 7.3 Growth-Adjusted Valuation -- 100 points (price-dependent)

Directly answers "is the price justified by expected growth?" -- the
project specification's core example (30% growth at 20x earnings vs. 30%
growth at 100x earnings) -- using two independent growth-adjusted
multiples so a company with weak or negative earnings is not unfairly
penalized by relying on P/E-derived PEG alone:

```
PEG-style           = P/E / (Forecast Revenue Growth, as a whole number e.g. 15)
EV/EBITDA-to-Growth = EV/EBITDA / (Forecast Revenue Growth, as a whole number)
```

| Subcomponent | Points | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| PEG-style (P/E to forecast growth) | 50 | 20.0+ -> 0 pts, 1.0 or below -> full pts (Peter Lynch's classic heuristic: a fairly priced company's P/E roughly equals its growth rate) |
| EV/EBITDA to forecast growth | 50 | 15.0+ -> 0 pts, 1.0 or below -> full pts |

**Calibration note:** the textbook PEG heuristic ("PEG of 3.0 is
expensive") assumes a punchy near-term consensus EPS-growth estimate, not
this project's conservative, 5-year-fading forecast revenue growth rate
(see Section 3). Computed against the latter, PEG values of 5-20 are the
NORM for real companies in this model, not a rare extreme -- a 3.0 floor
left almost every stock tested clamped to zero regardless of how
growth-adjusted-cheap it actually was. The floors above were set from the
empirical range this project's own formula produces (checked across
tickers spanning single digits to TSLA's ~85), so a merely-expensive case
is distinguishable from a truly extreme one.

### 7.4 Scenario-Weighted Risk/Reward -- 100 points (price-dependent)

Rather than scoring the Bull, Base, and Bear DCF cases independently
(which could reward a stock just for having a wide range of possible
outcomes), this blends them into a single probability-weighted expected
value using fixed, documented weights:

```
SCENARIO_PROBABILITIES = {bear: 25%, base: 50%, bull: 25%}
Weighted Value  = 0.25 x Bear + 0.50 x Base + 0.25 x Bull   (per-share intrinsic value)
Weighted Upside = (Weighted Value / Price) - 1
```

| Subcomponent | Points | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Probability-weighted expected upside | 60 | -100% -> 0 pts, +40% -> full pts |
| Bear-case downside severity | 25 | -130% -> 0 pts, 0% -> full pts |
| Upside / downside asymmetry ((Bull - Base) / (Base - Bear)) | 15 | 0.5x -> 0 pts, 3.0x -> full pts |

The asymmetry subcomponent directly implements the specification's
requirement to recognize when "there is meaningful potential upside, but
also meaningful downside" -- a stock is not rewarded merely for having
upside if its bear case is proportionally just as severe.

The ratio is built from the DIFFERENCES between scenario values -- (Bull -
Base) / (Base - Bear) -- not each scenario's upside relative to the
current price. A "Bull upside / Bear upside" ratio breaks down for a
company whose DCF is deeply overvalued even in the Bull case, since it
becomes a ratio of two negative numbers (meaningless, or sign-flipped).
The difference-based ratio stays well-defined regardless of whether the
absolute scenario values sit above or below the current price. It also
does not assume Bull > Base > Bear always holds: for a capital-intensive,
thin-margin business, a Bull scenario's higher revenue growth can require
MORE incremental CapEx/working-capital investment than the margin
improvement generates, making faster growth value-DESTRUCTIVE in the
model (confirmed against a real ticker in testing) and breaking that
ordering. When it breaks down, the subscore is left as a neutral "missing"
score rather than a misleadingly bad or good number.

### 7.5 Growth -- 70 points (price-independent, Business Quality)

| Historical EPS growth | 15 | 0% -> 0 pts, 15% -> full pts |

### 7.6 Profitability, Financial Health, Cash Flow Quality, Risk

These four categories are unchanged in methodology from a pure
fundamentals perspective, only rescaled to fit the new 250-point Business
Quality budget (Profitability/Financial Health 70 points each, Cash Flow
Quality 40 points) plus the still-100-point Risk category:

**Profitability -- 70 points:** Gross margin (10, 0%->0/70%->full),
Operating margin (15, 0%->0/30%->full), Net margin (10, 0%->0/25%->full),
ROE (15, 0%->0/30%->full), ROIC (15, 0%->0/25%->full), Margin stability
(5, lower std. dev. of operating margin -> more points; 0.20+ std. dev. ->

handful of legitimately high-margin-volatility businesses, e.g. a
hypergrowth semiconductor mid-cycle or a pharma name post-patent-cliff,
showed real std. dev. well above that without being "really awful").

**Financial Health -- 70 points:** Debt/Equity (15, 2.5x->0/0.0x->full),
Current ratio (10, 0.5x->0/2.5x->full), Net Debt/EBITDA (15,
5.0x->0/-1.0x->full), Debt/EBITDA (15, 5.0x->0/0.0x->full), Interest

**Cash Flow Quality -- 40 points:** FCF margin (12, 0%->0/20%->full), FCF

0.5x->0/1.5x->full), FCF consistency (8, full if positive every year, half
if positive at least half the time, zero otherwise).

**Risk -- 100 points (lower risk = MORE points):** Valuation sensitivity /
bull-bear dispersion (25), Debt risk / Net Debt-EBITDA (25), Earnings
volatility (20, 3.0+ std. dev. of EPS growth -> 0 pts, 0.0 -> full pts --
widened from an original 0.40 floor for the same reason as Margin
stability above), Margin volatility (15, same 0.20 floor as Margin
stability), and **Data confidence** (15) --
the fraction of all subscores across the whole model that were backed by
real data rather than the neutral half-credit default, directly
implementing the specification's call to weigh "confidence in the
assumptions."

### 7.7 Design guarantee: quality alone cannot dominate the score

Business Quality is capped at 250 of 1000 possible points. A company with
excellent growth, profitability, financial health, and cash flow (the
project specification's "Company A": 30% growth, 35% margins, 30% ROIC,
excellent balance sheet) cannot score above 250 on quality alone -- every
remaining point comes from the four price-dependent categories plus Risk,
all evaluated against the CURRENT price. If that price is unreasonable
relative to the DCF, comps, the company's own valuation history, and its
peers, those 750 points collapse toward zero and the total score lands in
Hold/Weak territory despite the excellent business -- exactly the
specification's worked example. Conversely, a good-but-not-exceptional
company ("Company B": 75/100 quality) trading at a genuine discount can
score highly because those same 750 points are earned independently of
how good the underlying business is. This is verified directly in
`tests/test_scoring.py::test_quality_alone_cannot_reach_strong_buy`.

### 7.9 Price Sensitivity Test

`price_sensitivity_table()` recomputes the four price-dependent categories
(7.1-7.4) at hypothetical prices 20%/10% above and below the actual
current price, while holding Business Quality and Risk fixed (since
neither depends on price), and reports the resulting total score and
rating at each hypothetical price. Business Quality and Risk are added
back in unchanged at every price so the same underlying function,
`_price_dependent_categories()`, is used both for the real score and for
every row of this table -- guaranteeing they can never drift out of sync.

The relationship between price and score is **never hard-coded**; it
emerges from the formulas in 7.1-7.4, all of which divide by (or otherwise
depend on) price. Whether a given company's score swings sharply or only
modestly across this range is itself informative: a company whose score
is dominated by Business Quality and Risk (both price-independent) will
show a smaller swing than one whose score is dominated by the
price-dependent categories.

### 7.10 Positive / Negative Factors

The "Why?" reasons shown on the results page are generated mechanically:
any subcomponent scoring >=75% of its available points is listed as a
positive factor; any subcomponent scoring <=30% is listed as a negative
factor. **Nothing is hand-written per company** -- the factors are a
direct readout of the computed subscores.

### 7.12 Two readouts that run in opposite directions

Two figures in the report are deliberately presented differently from the way
they are computed, and both are worth stating plainly.

**Risk is displayed as a risk LEVEL, but scored as safety.** The Risk category
contributes 100 points to the 1000-point total, and like every other category
those points are awarded for being good -- that is, for being safe. A company
with no debt and stable earnings earns close to 100 of them. Displaying that
number directly meant "Risk: 92/100" appeared next to a very conservative
business, which inverts what almost everyone takes the word to mean.

The headline readout therefore shows `100 - points`, so 0 means no measured
risk and 100 means maximum measured risk. This is a display transform only.
The points themselves keep their original direction, because reversing them
in the scoring would mean leverage and earnings volatility started pushing
companies *up* toward a Buy rating. The category breakdown still lists points
earned, and labels that row accordingly so the two numbers cannot be mistaken
for a contradiction.

**Potential Upside is the optimistic case, over one year, and never negative.**
`DCFResult.upside` is the total gap between the base-case intrinsic value and
today's price. It is frequently and legitimately negative, and it is a
multi-year thesis rather than a one-year move. It remains visible in the DCF
Valuation section, labelled "DCF vs. Current Price".

The headline "Potential Upside (1-Year)" answers a different question -- if
things go well, how much could this gain in a year? It takes whichever is
higher of:

1. the analyst consensus 12-month price target, which is by convention
   already a one-year figure and reflects forward information this tool
   cannot otherwise see; and
2. this project's own bull-case DCF, annualized over the forecast horizon,
   which covers companies with thin or no analyst coverage.

It is floored at zero, and reports which source it used. Relying on the DCF
alone was tried first and rejected: because that model is deliberately
conservative (median base-case upside of -54% across 49 real companies), it
reported 0.0% potential upside for Microsoft, Apple, NVIDIA and JPMorgan
simultaneously -- a far stronger claim than the model supports, and one
contradicted by the dozens of analysts covering each of them.

The zero floor conceals nothing. The base-case intrinsic value sits directly
beside the figure, so an overvalued company is plainly visible; the bear case
drives its own subscore; and the full scenario range appears in the scenario
table. When the figure does read 0.0%, the app says why in a caption beneath
it. It is an upside case and is labelled as one -- never an expected return,
a prediction, or a target.

## 8. Known Limitations

- **Historical-average CapEx/D&A assumptions can be distorted by a
  temporary capital-investment cycle.** For a company undergoing an
  unusually heavy, temporary capex buildout (for example, large
  AI-data-center capex in a recent fiscal year), the automatic DCF
  assumption -- which averages CapEx-to-revenue across all available
  historical years -- can carry that elevated capex intensity into the
  entire 5-year forecast and the terminal value, materially understating
  free cash flow and therefore intrinsic value relative to what an analyst
  using explicit forward capex guidance might model. Advanced users can
  override the CapEx % of Revenue assumption directly to correct for this.
- **A discounted cash flow model is a weaker fit for banks, insurers,
  broker-dealers, and other financial-services companies generally**,
  even with the FCF-masking fix in Section 2 applied. Standard equity
  research values these companies primarily on ROE, Price/Book, and
  dividend-paying capacity -- their business is fundamentally about
  managing a balance sheet of financial assets and liabilities (loans,
  deposits, premiums, investment portfolios), not converting revenue into
  free cash flow the way an operating company does. The DCF, WACC, and
  comparable-company sections still run for these companies (and the
  comparable-company cross-check is the more reliable of the two for
  this class of business), but the DCF output in particular should be
  weighted less heavily for a bank or insurer than for an industrial or
  technology company.
- **Leverage ratios run structurally higher for an industrial company
  with a large captive finance subsidiary** (e.g. Ford Motor Credit, GM
  Financial). The subsidiary borrows to fund customer auto loans/leases --
  a normal, by-design characteristic of that business model -- and its
  debt consolidates onto the parent's balance sheet alongside the
  industrial operations' own debt. Found in testing: Ford's Debt/EBITDA
  and Net Debt/EBITDA computed as roughly 40-46x, driven by both this
  consolidation effect and a genuine recent earnings decline (2025 EBITDA
  fell to ~$3.6B from ~$14B the prior year) -- both real, correctly
  computed numbers, not a data error, but the tool cannot separate the
  industrial and financing segments from the data available to say how
  much of the ratio is "structural" versus a genuine credit-quality signal.
- Financial statement history is limited to whatever the free data
  provider makes available (typically ~4 years).
- The sector-based peer list is a simplifying, transparent proxy for a
  professional peer-screening service; it does not account for
  differences in business model, growth stage, or company size within a
  sector.
- All forecasts are inherently uncertain; the sensitivity and scenario
  analyses exist specifically to surface that uncertainty rather than
  hide it behind a single point estimate.
- Yahoo Finance data can occasionally be delayed, incomplete, or
  temporarily unavailable (rate limits). The app is designed to degrade to
  "N/A" for any specific missing metric rather than fail entirely, but a
  fully unavailable ticker will raise a clear on-screen error.
- This tool performs quantitative analysis only. It does not incorporate
  qualitative factors such as management quality, litigation, regulatory
  risk, or competitive dynamics.
- **Expected Return at Current Price (Section 7.2) is a simplified
  decomposition, not a precise IRR.** It assumes the company's EV/EBITDA
  multiple reverts toward the current peer-group median over the forecast
  horizon -- a documented, reasonable, but ultimately assumed reversion
  target, since neither history nor certainty exists for where a multiple
  "should" trade. If no reliable peer multiple is available, the model
  assumes zero multiple change rather than guessing.
- **The historical valuation percentile (Section 7.1) is limited by
  statement history depth.** With ~4-5 years of trailing P/E data points,
  the percentile ranking is a useful but statistically thin sample --
  it answers "cheap or rich vs. recent history," not "cheap or rich vs.
  a full market cycle."

## 9. Speculation Score (0-1000) (`src/speculative.py`, `src/themes.py`)

### 9.0 The question this score answers

> **"How much potential does this stock have to EXPLODE in value if its
> bullish thesis, catalysts, or emerging-industry opportunity plays out --
> and how credible is that scenario?"**

This is deliberately **not** the question the Fundamental Investment Score
(Section 7) answers. That score asks whether the stock is attractive to buy
at today's price given its current financial condition. The Speculation
Score asks how large the future could be.

The two scores are **independent by design and are expected to disagree**:

| Profile | Fundamental | Speculation | Is this a contradiction? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Emerging-tech company, unprofitable, expensive, huge future market | 350 | 900 | **No.** Poor as a conventional investment today; enormous asymmetric upside if the thesis works. |
| Mature, profitable, reasonably priced, dominant in a settled industry | 850 | 200 | **No.** An excellent investment with almost no path to becoming several times larger. |

Neither pairing is an error. They are two different questions about the same
company, and the app displays them together, never merged into one number.

### 9.1 Three things this score deliberately does NOT do

**1. It does not penalize a high valuation multiple.** No P/E, EV/EBITDA,
EV/Sales, PEG or peer-multiple comparison appears anywhere in the eight
categories below. Expensiveness is a Fundamental Score concern.

Price enters the Speculation Score through exactly **one** channel: market
capitalization, inside the "current market cap -> potential market cap"
ratio in Category 5. That is a statement about **scale**, not about
valuation. A $4T company genuinely cannot become 10x larger as easily as a
$4B one, regardless of how cheap or expensive either looks. Verified by
`test_a_high_valuation_multiple_is_not_penalized`, which changes a company's
reported P/E, forward P/E, P/S, EV/Revenue and EV/EBITDA and asserts the
score does not move at all.

**2. It does not reward volatility.** Beta, scenario dispersion, short
interest, options activity and retail popularity appear in **no formula**.
Volatility is not upside potential -- a stock that swings 10% a day has told
you nothing about the size of its future market. An earlier version of this
module scored beta and bull/bear dispersion; both were removed in the
redesign. Verified by `test_volatility_and_beta_play_no_part_in_the_score`,
which sets beta to 4.5 and asserts an identical score, and additionally
asserts that no subscore label anywhere contains "beta", "volatil",
"dispersion" or "short interest".

**3. It does not invent catalysts.** This tool has no access to news, press
releases, clinical-trial calendars or contract announcements, and it never
claims a specific company has a specific event scheduled. What it does
instead is name the **catalyst archetypes** that apply to a company's
industry -- "NRC design certification milestones", "Phase 3 data readouts",
"logical-qubit error-correction milestones" -- drawn from the table in
`src/themes.py` and labeled in the UI as industry-level catalyst *types*.

### 9.2 How forward-looking information enters the model

Financial statements are backward-looking. They cannot tell you whether a
company sits in front of a $10B market or a $1T one, and that question is
central to the score.

Every forward-looking assumption therefore lives in **one auditable file,
`src/themes.py`**, with a source note per industry. `src/speculative.py`
contains no industry constants of its own -- it reads them all from that
table. Disagree with a market-size estimate? Change it in one place and
every score updates consistently.

Each industry profile carries:

| Field | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `tam_usd` | Estimated addressable market at a ~2033-2035 horizon |
| `industry_cagr` | Estimated annualized industry growth to that horizon |
| `commercial_maturity` | 0-1: how commercially proven the industry is *today* |
| `breakthrough_potential` | 0-1: how much a technical breakthrough could re-rate the category |
| `catalyst_intensity` | 0-1: how catalyst-dense the industry is |
| `winner_take_most` | 0-1: how concentrated the eventual economics are |
| `plausible_winner_share` | Share of TAM a major winner could plausibly capture |
| `winner_ev_sales` | EV/Sales a successful company might command at that horizon |
| `catalyst_archetypes` | The *kinds* of events that reprice this industry |
| `source_note` | Where the estimate came from, in plain language |

**What these numbers are, stated honestly.** They are approximate,
mid-range figures drawn from widely published market-research and consulting
forecasts. Different research houses publish estimates differing by 2-5x for
the same emerging industry; where sources disagreed, a mid-range figure was
chosen deliberately rather than the largest available headline number. They
are **order-of-magnitude model assumptions, not facts or forecasts**, which
is why the scoring code consumes them on a **log scale** -- the difference
between a $50B and a $500B market is meaningful; the difference between
$90B and $110B is noise.

### 9.3 Industry classification, and the two gates against hype

A company is matched to an industry profile in `classify_theme()`:

1. **Explicit ticker mapping** (strongest) -- the ticker is listed in a
   theme's `tickers` tuple.
2. **Weighted keyword match** -- keywords are matched against the company's
   `industry`, `sector`, `longName`/`shortName` (weight 3, because these
   short fields state what a company *is*) and its business summary
   (weight 1 per distinct keyword). A **minimum score of 2** is required.
3. **No qualifying match** -> the `MATURE_INDUSTRY` fallback profile.

**Gate 1 -- the keyword threshold.** Requiring a score of 2 means a single
passing mention buried in a long business summary is never sufficient. This
threshold exists because of a real false positive found in testing: Exxon
Mobil's business description lists *"low-carbon data center"* among a dozen
other business lines, which matched the AI-infrastructure theme on one
incidental phrase and reclassified an integrated oil major as an AI company
with a 4.9x upside multiple. The threshold rejects that case without any
company-specific special-casing, and the case is now pinned as a regression
test (`test_incidental_keyword_mention_does_not_reclassify_a_company`).
A company whose own `industry` field names the technology still qualifies on
one match, since that field is definitional rather than incidental.

**Gate 2 -- the evidence requirement (`theme_evidence_strength`).** Matching
a theme is a claim about a business *description*. This function asks the
independent question: *is money actually moving in a way consistent with
that claim?* It averages whichever of five signals are available:

| Signal | Normalization | What it establishes |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue scale | $1M -> 0.0, $1B -> 1.0 (log) | Has it commercialized anything? |
| Revenue growth | 0% -> 0.0, 40% -> 1.0 | Is commercialization accelerating? |
| R&D intensity | 0% -> 0.0, 15% of revenue -> 1.0 | Is it really investing in the technology? |
| Gross margin | 0% -> 0.0, 60% -> 1.0 | Are the product economics real? |
| Capital investment | 0% -> 0.0, 15% of revenue -> 1.0 | Is it building capacity? |

This gates Categories 3 and 7, so a shell company with a trendy business
summary but no revenue, no R&D and no capital spending receives only **35%**
of its industry's raw optionality, while a company whose spending and
revenue genuinely look like a participant receives 100%. This is the direct
implementation of the requirement *"do not automatically give a high score
simply because a company uses a trendy keyword."* Verified by
`test_keyword_alone_cannot_manufacture_a_high_score`.

The evidence figure is displayed in the UI as "Corroborating evidence".

### 9.4 Category weights

| Category | Points | Measures |
|---|---:|---|
| TAM / Future Market Opportunity | 150 | How large could the addressable market become? |
| Explosive Growth Potential | 175 | Could the business grow many times over? |
| Industry / Technology Optionality | 150 | Could a breakthrough transform its value? |
| Catalyst Potential | 125 | How likely is a discrete repricing event? |
| 5-10 Year Asymmetric Upside | 150 | How many times larger could it become? |
| Probability of Becoming a Major Winner | 100 | Is the bullish scenario actually reachable? |
| Competitive / First-Mover Advantage | 75 | Could it be one of the few that captures the economics? |
| Market Mispricing / Underappreciated Potential | 75 | How much of the opportunity is not yet priced? |
| **TOTAL** | **1000** | |

The three largest weights (Growth 175, TAM 150, Asymmetric Upside 150) are
the forward-looking opportunity measures, which is what makes the score
opportunity-driven rather than risk-driven.

### 9.5 Category 1 -- TAM / Future Market Opportunity (150)

| Subscore | Points | Scale |
|---|---:|---|
| Future market size relative to current revenue | 60 | log, 3x -> 0, 2000x -> full |
| Estimated industry growth rate | 50 | 3% -> 0, 35% -> full |
| Absolute size of the future market | 40 | log, $20B -> 0, $500B -> full |

The first subscore is the heart of the category: a company earning $40M
against a $90B future market has thousands of times more headroom than one
earning $50B against a $500B market.

The absolute-size scale tops out at $500B rather than at the largest TAM in
the table because above roughly half a trillion dollars every market is
unambiguously enormous; stretching the scale to $1T+ would flatten all the
focused emerging industries (quantum, eVTOL, gene editing) against the few
broad ones, discriminating on a difference that carries no information for
this question.

### 9.6 Category 2 -- Explosive Growth Potential (175)

| Subscore | Points | Scale |
|---|---:|---|
| Demonstrated revenue growth (best of latest year / multi-year CAGR) | 60 | 0% -> 0, 60% -> full |
| Modeled bull-case sustained growth (5-year average) | 45 | 5% -> 0, 40% -> full |
| Room to multiply from a small revenue base | 40 | log, $50B -> 0, $50M -> full |
| Gross margin (scalability of growth into profit) | 30 | 10% -> 0, 75% -> full |

The largest weight goes to **actual, demonstrated** growth rather than a
projection, so the category is anchored in real data. The bull-case
subscore, drawn from the Section 5 scenario DCF, is a deliberate
conservative anchor -- see the limitation noted in 9.13.

**On the small-base subscore:** this responds to **revenue**, not market
capitalization. A small-cap with no revenue momentum gains nothing from
merely being small; the requirement is that revenue is small *and* growing.
10x from $50M is a far more ordinary event than 10x from $50B, and this is
the only place company size as such is rewarded.

### 9.7 Category 3 -- Industry / Technology Optionality (150)

| Subscore | Points | Formula |
|---|---:|---|
| Technological breakthrough potential | 90 | `breakthrough_potential x gate x 90` |
| Remaining optionality (how early the industry still is) | 60 | `commercial_maturity` 0.90 -> 0, 0.10 -> full, then `x gate` |

where `gate = 0.35 + 0.65 x evidence_strength` (Section 9.3, Gate 2).

The second subscore rewards **early** commercial maturity, because a settled
industry has little left to re-rate. That measures remaining optionality,
not quality -- the corresponding risk that an immature industry never
commercializes at all is priced separately in Category 6.

### 9.8 Category 4 -- Catalyst Potential (125)

| Subscore | Points | Scale |
|---|---:|---|
| Industry catalyst density | 45 | `catalyst_intensity` 0.15 -> 0, 0.95 -> full |
| Capacity build-out pace (CapEx growth) | 40 | 0% -> 0, 60% -> full |
| Approach to a profitability inflection (operating-margin improvement) | 40 | 0 -> 0, +25pp -> full |

The two company-specific subscores are measurable **precursors**, not
predictions: factories, fabs, data centers and launch facilities are built
before they generate revenue, so a sharply rising capital-expenditure line
genuinely precedes new capacity coming online; and a rapidly improving
operating margin means the company is approaching the profitability
crossover, historically one of the most powerful single repricing events for
a growth company.

### 9.9 Category 5 -- 5-10 Year Asymmetric Upside (150)

The most important single category, and the one that implements *"if this
company becomes a major winner, how much larger could it become?"*

```
potential revenue    = TAM  x  plausible winner's share  x  positioning factor
                       (floored at current revenue -- success does not involve shrinking)
potential market cap = potential revenue  x  winner's EV/Sales
upside multiple      = potential market cap / current market cap
```

`positioning factor = 0.25 + 1.75 x evidence_strength`, ranging from 0.25x
for a weak participant with almost no corroborating evidence to 2.0x for a
demonstrably dominant one. This is what prevents every company in an
industry from sharing one identical upside estimate.

| Subscore | Points | Scale |
|---|---:|---|
| Potential upside if the bullish thesis succeeds | 100 | log, 1.5x -> 0, 15x -> full |
| Upside-to-downside asymmetry | 50 | log, 1x -> 0, 20x -> full |

The asymmetry subscore is `(upside multiple - 1) / downside severity`, where
downside severity is `1 - net cash / market cap`, clamped to [0.40, 1.00]. A
company holding cash worth a meaningful fraction of its market value has a
harder floor beneath it, so the same 10x upside represents better
risk-reward. This is why a well-funded speculative company outranks an
identically positioned but nearly insolvent one.

### 9.10 Category 6 -- Probability of Becoming a Major Winner (100)

**This category is the brake.** Speculation is not imagination: a company
with a vast theoretical opportunity but no evidence it can execute must not
reach the top bands on hope alone.

| Subscore | Points | Scale |
|---|---:|---|
| Commercial traction (revenue) | 25 | log, $1M -> 0, $2B -> full |
| Funding adequacy (years of cash runway) | 30 | 0.5yr -> 0, 4yr -> full |
| Product economics (gross margin) | 20 | 0% -> 0, 65% -> full |
| R&D scale in absolute dollars | 15 | log, $5M -> 0, $2B -> full |
| Dilution discipline (share-count growth) | 10 | +35%/yr -> 0, 0% -> full |

Cash runway carries the largest weight because it is the single most common
cause of permanent loss in speculative names: a great thesis is worthless to
existing holders after a rescue financing at a 70% discount. R&D is scored
in **absolute dollars** here (intensity says a company tries hard; absolute
spend says whether it can realistically outbuild rivals spending a hundred
times more), and separately as intensity in Category 7 -- two different
questions, not double-counting.

### 9.11 Category 7 -- Competitive / First-Mover Advantage (75)

Patents, network effects and switching costs cannot be read from financial
statements, so this uses the measurable shadows they cast:

| Subscore | Points | Scale |
|---|---:|---|
| Winner-take-most industry structure | 25 | `winner_take_most x (0.40 + 0.60 x evidence)` |
| Pricing power (gross margin) | 20 | 15% -> 0, 70% -> full |
| Market-share gains (revenue growth *minus its own industry's* growth) | 20 | -15pp -> 0, +40pp -> full |
| Technology-leadership investment (R&D / revenue) | 10 | 2% -> 0, 30% -> full |

The share-gains subscore is the cleanest available evidence of competitive
strength: growing slower than your own market means losing ground however
fast you grow.

### 9.12 Category 8 -- Market Mispricing / Underappreciated Potential (75)

| Subscore | Points | Scale |
|---|---:|---|
| Current revenue as a fraction of the future market | 30 | log, 5x -> 0, 1000x -> full |
| Fraction of the successful-case value already in the price | 30 | 60% -> 0, 2% -> full |
| Analyst consensus price target vs. current price | 15 | -10% -> 0, +60% -> full |

This is **not** a valuation check. It asks how far today's market value sits
from what the successful outcome would be worth. A company at 2% of its
successful-case value has an enormous unpriced gap; one already at 80% has
largely been paid for in advance. That is a statement about remaining
opportunity, not about expensiveness.

The first subscore captures the related idea that when current revenue is a
vanishingly small fraction of the future market, conventional
revenue-multiple frameworks simply cannot express the opportunity, so the
market is likely valuing the company on an outdated basis.

### 9.13 Rating bands

| Score | Rating |
|---|---|
| 951-1000 | Once-in-a-Generation Speculative Opportunity |
| 851-950 | Exceptional / Explosive Speculation |
| 751-850 | Extreme Speculation |
| 601-750 | Very High Speculation |
| 451-600 | High Speculation |
| 301-450 | Moderate Speculation |
| 151-300 | Low Speculation |
| 0-150 | Minimal Speculative Upside |

The two top bands are deliberately narrow and hard to reach: clearing 851
requires strength across nearly every category simultaneously, not just
large upside potential. Pinned exactly by
`test_rating_bands_match_the_specified_scale`.

### 9.14 Required explanation above 700

Any score above **700** generates a structured, five-part explanation
(`SpeculativeExplanation`), rendered prominently in the UI:

1. Why the opportunity is potentially explosive
2. The future industry / TAM driving it
3. The catalyst types that apply to that industry
4. The potential upside scenario, with the full arithmetic
5. The biggest reason the thesis could fail

Above **850**, an additional explicit qualification states why the company
clears the "explosive" bar.

Every sentence is a template with computed figures substituted in. There is
no free-form generated prose and no claim the tool has not measured. Part 5
is chosen mechanically as the **weakest-scoring category**, so it is always
the model's own most negative finding rather than a token caveat.

### 9.15 Missing data, and Speculative Confidence

Identical to the Fundamental Score's policy (Section 7): an unavailable
input scores neutral half-credit and is flagged `missing=True` with
`value=NaN`. It is never treated as zero. **The score reflects the
opportunity, not the completeness of the data feed.**

A genuine zero or negative number is real data and is preserved with its
sign -- negative dilution (net buybacks) scores as the favourable value it
is. `_log_score()` makes this explicit: a non-positive value sits at the
bottom of an ascending scale (or the top of a descending one) *without*
being flagged missing; only NaN is missing.

**Speculative Confidence (0-100)** is the fraction of subscores computed
from available data. It measures confidence in *this analysis*, not the
probability that the bullish thesis succeeds -- a distinct concept, labeled
as such in the UI.

### 9.16 The score is not a probability

A 900 does not mean a 90% chance of anything. It means the combination of
market size, growth potential, optionality, catalysts, credibility and
asymmetry ranks in the top tier of what this model measures. The UI states
this explicitly wherever the score appears.

### 9.17 Known limitations

1. **The TAM estimates are the model's largest single assumption.** They are
   published third-party forecasts for emerging industries, which have a
   poor historical track record. They are disclosed as estimates with source
   notes, consumed on a log scale to limit their leverage, and centralized
   in one editable file precisely so a user can substitute their own.

2. **Industry classification is keyword- and ticker-based**, not a true
   business classifier. The threshold in 9.3 rejects incidental mentions,
   but a company whose theme exposure is real yet described in unusual
   language may fall back to `MATURE_INDUSTRY`. The classification and its
   basis are always displayed, so a miss is visible rather than silent.

3. **The bull-case growth subscore inherits the DCF's conservatism.** The
   Section 5 scenario engine is mean-reverting by design, which is correct
   for valuation but understates plausible growth for a hypergrowth
   pre-commercial company. This is why that subscore carries only 45 of
   1000 points and the larger 60-point weight goes to demonstrated growth.

4. **No qualitative research.** No news, filings, patent databases, clinical
   calendars, management assessment or contract announcements. Categories
   that a human analyst would answer qualitatively (catalysts, competitive
   moat) are answered here with disclosed quantitative proxies, named
   explicitly in each subscore label.

5. **`plausible_winner_share` and `winner_ev_sales` are judgment calls.**
   They set how large "success" means, and they are per-industry constants
   in `src/themes.py` rather than company-specific analysis.

### 9.18 Verification

Beyond the unit tests, the score was validated across a deliberately diverse
set of real companies -- emerging-tech small caps, mega-cap technology,
biotech, EV, fintech, and mature blue chips -- with **no expected ratings
hard-coded anywhere**. The resulting spread separates cleanly along exactly
the axis the score is meant to measure:

* **Extreme Speculation (751-850):** small/mid-cap companies in large,
  early-stage emerging industries with real corroborating evidence.
* **Very High / High (451-750):** established emerging-tech companies,
  clinical-stage biotech, and pre-revenue names whose upside is real but
  whose evidence or industry maturity holds them back.
* **Moderate (301-450):** large-cap technology in growing but commercially
  settled industries.
* **Low / Minimal (0-300):** mature blue chips and mega-caps -- scoring low
  for the correct reason (limited potential to become several times larger),
  not because of any data gap. A $4T company cannot 10x; a $650B integrated
  oil major has no emerging-industry exposure.

Notably, the largest and most profitable companies score *lowest* here while
scoring highest on the Fundamental Score -- the clearest possible
demonstration that the two scores are genuinely independent.
